Memory

Learning a word: why thinking about meaning helps

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • The processing you do counts for more than how the exercise looks.
  • An ambiguous context calls for a check rather than prolonged guessing.
  • A list and a sentence can support complementary goals.

A sentence can do more than decorate a card. It can pin down the meaning of a word, show how it is built into a sentence and help you understand why a translation fits here but not elsewhere. You still have to read and understand it: having an example there doesn’t, on its own, guarantee deep processing.

What depth of processing means

Craik and Lockhart (1972) proposed a framework that focuses on the kind of operations carried out during study. Looking at the shape of a word, judging a rhyme and thinking about its meaning don’t involve the same processing. The framework guided experiments; it is not a universal scale on which sound and spelling would always matter less than meaning.

In their ten experiments published in 1975, Craik and Tulving asked students to make physical, phonological or semantic judgments about words before a memory test. Semantic judgments generally improved retention in these tasks. The experiments also looked at processing time, elaboration and how well the question matched the answer.

This result doesn’t directly compare “a single English sentence” with “ten readings of a bilingual list”. Nor does it allow anyone to promise that a longer sentence will double your learning. What counts is the operation you carry out and what the final test asks for.

An example: understanding brittle

The old paper was brittle and cracked when she unfolded it. Here, the paper breaks easily: brittle means hard but easily broken, close to “fragile”. The verb cracked and the action of unfolding make the physical property clear.

You could just write down “brittle = fragile”. But the sentence lets you ask a better question: what kind of fragility are we talking about? The word can also have figurative uses, for example for a relationship or a tone of voice; this first sentence doesn’t cover all its meanings.

Useful processing means connecting the words and explaining the choice, not gazing at the example. If unfolded is new to you, look at its translation. A context you can’t follow at all is not automatically a beneficial difficulty.

Infer, then check

Guessing a meaning from context is real work, but context doesn’t always give a single answer. In She made a remarkably candid comment, you can tell that the comment stands out without knowing whether candid means honest, funny or harsh. A sentence doesn’t necessarily contain enough cues.

Form a hypothesis, then check it in an explanation or a reliable dictionary. Then go back to the sentence: does the confirmed meaning fit? This last step stops you from holding on to a first guess that is attractive but wrong.

The experiments by van den Broek and colleagues (2022) compare chances to infer and chances to retrieve during reading, first with teenagers and then with adults. With teenagers, guessing the meaning from context worked better than having to remember it; with adults, who were better prepared and received corrections, the two were equal. The study invites you to look at the quality of the help, rather than always setting “guessing” against “testing yourself”.

A list can also make you think

The contrast between “active context” and “passive list” is misleading. A bilingual list can be used to recall a translation from memory, compare two shades of meaning or check a spelling. A sentence, on the other hand, can be read without attention, with the answer already visible.

The format makes some operations easier; it doesn’t ensure them. For the written form, you also need to look at the letters. For pronunciation, you need to listen and produce the sound. A meaning-focused activity doesn’t replace all the other aspects of knowing a word.

A short routine focused on meaning

Take a word that interests you and a sentence you can understand. Explain in your own language what is happening. Identify the meaning of the word in this situation. Then hide the English form and try to retrieve it. Finally, change one element of the example: the person, the object or the situation.

With reliable, moving from a reliable colleague to a reliable source has you practice a new use without inventing an overly complex sentence. If you are not sure about your wording, have it checked. This is only a suggestion for practice.

Frequently asked questions

Is a sentence always better than a list?
No. It depends on the activity, the quality of the context and what is being assessed. A list can also train demanding retrieval.
Should you always guess the meaning before looking it up?
No. An inference can help if the context offers useful cues. Then check the meaning; an uncorrected mistake is not the goal.
