Desirable difficulties: when effort helps you learn

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • A difficulty is worth having if it helps a relevant later performance.
  • Confusion and misunderstood instructions are not teaching benefits.
  • Spacing, retrieving and mixing call for different decisions.

A word that takes effort isn’t necessarily better learned. A word you retrieve easily isn’t necessarily badly learned. The idea of a “desirable difficulty” is useful precisely because it makes you tell apart a difficulty that serves the task from one that gets in its way.

Don’t confuse immediate ease with lasting learning

In their 2011 overview chapter, Elizabeth and Robert Bjork describe conditions that can slow apparent progress during study while improving later retention or transfer. They mention in particular spacing, retrieval, and certain forms of variation and interleaving.

This is a theoretical framework backed by experiments, not a law saying that all hardship is beneficial. The authors stress that the learner’s knowledge and skills determine whether a difficulty is useful. Something that makes one person think can leave another with no way of answering.

The ideas of “storage strength” and “retrieval strength” belong to this model. No app measures them directly in the brain. Presenting storage as an archive from which no memory could ever disappear would turn a modeling hypothesis into a biological certainty.

A demonstration, not a universal rule

In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), repeatedly rereading texts led to better results on an almost immediate test, while repeated retrieval led to better results on a delayed test. The result shows that an assessment after a few minutes and one after a week can rank the same methods differently.

It doesn’t prove that fluency is always misleading, or that hesitation is always productive. If you know a common word well, retrieving it quickly is exactly the skill you want. Difficulty is not the end goal: sometimes it is a means of training a skill.

Three situations for judging effort

You understand the sentence but are searching for the word. In The instructions were clear and easy to follow, hiding instructions asks you for recall that is within your reach. The clue helps you work out the meaning without necessarily giving away the English form. This is one possible way to work on the word.

You understand almost nothing in the sentence. If the pattern and several words are unknown, keeping the answer hidden doesn’t ensure better learning. Read the translation, clarify the meaning and, if needed, choose a simpler example. Removing this difficulty is not “cheating”.

You mix up two words. For borrow and lend, compare the roles: Can I borrow your pen? and Can you lend me your pen? One person receives something for a while, the other gives it for a while. Comparing them explicitly can help more than going over the two translations without understanding the difference.

These examples guarantee nothing: they help you see what each task asks of you.

Spacing and mixing are different decisions

Spacing organizes returns over time. Interleaving alternates different material. The advantage of mixing observed in mathematics doesn’t automatically carry over to vocabulary lists: results change with the material.

So don’t make every session random on principle. You can first understand a word in a clear situation, then retrieve it among others. You can also compare two similar uses when the confusion itself becomes what you are working on.

When to reduce the difficulty

Lower the obstacle when you don’t know what is expected, when the example gives you no way into the meaning, or when the same mistakes keep coming back without a correction you understand. A cue, a translation or a grammar explanation can then support the next recall.

Adding more new words or stretching out a tiring session doesn’t necessarily create a desirable difficulty. The time you have and your willingness to come back count. A routine modest enough to stay manageable is worth more than a difficulty chosen only to look demanding.

Using this well in Vocalibur

Cards offer several kinds of help and ways of answering. Use them according to the aspect you want to work on: meaning, written form or spoken recall. Search before tapping “Reveal” when you can, then swipe according to what you had really retrieved: right (“I know it”) or left (“To review”). Neither the swipe nor the feeling of effort is, on its own, a measure of learning.

The question to keep in mind is concrete: after checking, do you understand the answer better, and will you be able to try it again? That is a better test than “was this session hard enough?”.

Frequently asked questions

Is a harder exercise always better for learning?
No. A difficulty you can’t get past, or one unrelated to the goal, can hinder learning. The effort should allow an attempt you understand and a check.
Should you avoid looking at the answer?
No. Try first when the task is within reach, then use the answer to check or clarify. The point is not to stay stuck indefinitely.
