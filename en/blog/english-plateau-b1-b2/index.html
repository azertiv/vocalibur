Learning English

Stuck at B1 or B2 in English? Find what’s blocking you

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • The word “plateau” covers several possible difficulties.
  • The right exercise depends on the task that resists you, not only on a B1 or B2 label.
  • Improvement on cards and improvement in conversation have to be observed separately.

A plateau is first an impression to examine

You understand the main points, but fast conversations get away from you. Your sentences work, but always seem to be built from the same words. You feel you are no longer moving forward. This doesn’t prove that your memory has reached a limit, or that there is a biological block specific to B1.

The word “plateau” covers different situations: progress that is hard to notice, goals that have become more demanding, practice that has become too repetitive or a skill that gets little training. You need to identify which one applies to you before increasing the amount of work.

Go from a label to a difficulty you can observe

The CEFR scale describes, among other things, a wider range of situations understood and greater ease between B1 and B2. It doesn’t define a quota of words whose acquisition would trigger the move to the next level.

Look at a real task. During a short video, are you stuck on the words themselves, their pronunciation, the speed of speech or the links between ideas? In a work message, is it vocabulary, sentence structure or organization that slows you down? “I don’t have enough words” is sometimes right, but it isn’t an automatic diagnosis.

Three situations, three different responses

You know the word in writing, but not when you hear it

Take a short clip with a transcript. Listen first without reading, then compare what you heard with the text. Find the few parts you missed and listen to them again. This activity targets recognizing sounds. Adding only written cards would risk training an ability that is already fairly solid.

You understand, but search for your words

Work from the idea you want to express, not only from the English word to translate. The work of Webb (2009), on word pairs with learners in Japan, shows that the direction of learning affects the knowledge gained. This supports adapting the exercise to the task, without showing that a productive card will be enough to make a conversation fluent.

For example, prepare three useful expressions for explaining that you disagree. Retrieve them without a model, then use them in a short message of your own. Have the wording checked when you are unsure whether it sounds natural.

You get stuck on a specialized topic

Choose accessible material from that field and note the words you really need to understand it. A topic is relevant when it meets your own use. Piling up rare terms because they seem “advanced”, on the other hand, can draw you away from the vocabulary you miss every week.

A short cycle of observation

Here is an idea to adapt: for two weeks, keep a single priority and two short starting points. For example, a one-minute recording of you telling a story about an event and a few notes on the words you were missing. Work on a handful of phrasings you can reuse, then record another story of similar difficulty.

Listen for what has changed: are the words more available? Are your hesitations about something else? If nothing moves, adjust the difficulty or ask for outside feedback. Successful cards and easier speaking are two different observations. One doesn’t automatically prove the other.

Frequently asked questions

Is the B1 plateau a biological limit?
Noticing that you have stalled doesn’t support that conclusion. You need to look at your goals, your practice and the way you measure progress.
Is learning more rare words enough?
No. Specialized words can be useful in a specific field, but they don’t automatically solve a difficulty with listening, sentence structure or interaction.
