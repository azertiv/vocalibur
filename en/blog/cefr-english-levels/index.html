Learning English

CEFR English levels A1 to C2 explained

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • The CEFR describes language abilities, not vocabulary quotas.
  • A profile can differ between writing and speaking, or from one field to another.
  • A content level in an app is not a personal certification.

A level describes what you can do

Understanding an official letter, telling a story from your past and taking part in a debate don’t call for exactly the same resources. The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, or CEFR, provides reference points for describing these skills. It doesn’t sort people by an official number of memorized words.

The Council of Europe’s global scale distinguishes six levels. The table below gives a short rewording of it, not a grid good enough to assess yourself or certify a level.

Simplified reference points from the CEFR global scale
LevelExamples of expected abilities
A1Exchange simple personal information, with someone who speaks clearly and helps when needed.
A2Communicate in everyday situations and describe your surroundings or needs in simple terms.
B1Follow the main points of clear speech on a familiar topic and give a connected account of an experience.
B2Understand the main ideas of complex material and develop a point of view with some ease.
C1Understand demanding texts, pick up implicit meaning and express yourself flexibly in a range of contexts.
C2Summarize information from different sources and express fine shades of meaning, even in complex situations.

C2 doesn’t mean “knowing every word”, or expressing yourself without a single mistake. The text of the framework states that this level doesn’t aim to reproduce an ideal native speaker. Precision, ease and adapting to the situation count for more than an image of perfection.

Your profile can be uneven without being inconsistent

You may read technical articles with confidence and still search for words on the phone. Your work may have given you specialized vocabulary that doesn’t cover everyday conversation. A single overall label sums up this profile; it doesn’t show all its ups and downs.

To choose your next goal, describe an observable difficulty instead: “I understand the summary but not the argument”, “I can prepare a presentation but I hesitate when it comes to questions”, or “I recognize the vocabulary but can’t retrieve it when speaking”. This diagnosis leads to a more precise activity than “going up a level”.

Why a word counter doesn’t replace an assessment

A vocabulary test can shed light on part of the profile, as long as it explains what it counts and how it was validated. Recognizing a meaning among four answers measures neither listening, nor grammar, nor how a text is organized. The different meanings of a word can also be known unevenly.

An app’s difficulty labels are there to organize its content. They don’t mean that each entry is official, or that learning all the cards in a set is enough to reach the corresponding level. For a school, university or work requirement, use an assessment suited to the skills required and to the standards of the organization concerned.

Turn the framework into your next step

Take a document you would like to understand: an article, a short video or a set of instructions. Note what is really getting in the way. If it is a few recurring words, work on them. If you know the words but lose the thread, practice spotting the structure. If the problem only appears when listening, working on written cards won’t be enough.

Frequently asked questions

Does C2 mean speaking perfectly, like a native speaker?
No. The framework describes great precision and ease, not an ideal native speaker or a complete absence of mistakes.
Can a vocabulary quiz certify my level?
No. It doesn’t cover all the skills the CEFR describes. A certification requires a suitable assessment.
