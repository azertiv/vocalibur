How long does it take to learn English?

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Cambridge’s hours are rough figures for class hours, counted from the very beginning.
  • They can’t be replaced by the same amount of time spent on cards.
  • A concrete goal and a suitable assessment are worth more than a guaranteed date.

A length of time only makes sense with a destination

“Learning English” can mean booking a room, reading research articles or working in the language every day. The time needed therefore depends on the skill you are aiming for, your starting point and your chances to practice. A single promise, expressed in days or in minutes of app use, wipes out these differences.

The right first question is: what task do you want to be able to do more easily? Describing a concrete goal then lets you choose a way to practice and a way to observe progress.

Cambridge’s hours: a reference point, not a countdown

Cambridge English publishes approximate numbers of guided learning hours needed to reach each CEFR level when starting from zero. These are classes and supervised study, not the total time of all exposure to English.

Guided hours counted from the very beginning, according to Cambridge English
Target levelApproximate range
A190 to 100 hours
A2180 to 200 hours
B1350 to 400 hours
B2500 to 600 hours
C1700 to 800 hours
C21,000 to 1,200 hours

These figures are cumulative: the B2 row doesn’t mean adding 500 to 600 hours after B1. Cambridge points out in particular the influence of previous experience, intensity, age and exposure outside class. So subtracting two figures from the table doesn’t give a precise individual prediction for moving from one level to the next.

Why fifteen minutes of cards don’t become fifteen minutes of class

A card session can train you to retrieve a word. A class can also work on interaction, listening, pronunciation, writing and correcting mistakes. The content and the measures can’t be swapped for one another. Dividing Cambridge’s hours by your daily review time would give a date that looks precise but has no basis.

In the same way, the number of consecutive days of use shows regularity, not a level. The length of a session doesn’t, on its own, tell you whether you have learned. You need to look at what you can retrieve or do later.

Plan without inventing a universal speed

Here is an idea to adapt, not a validated procedure: for two weeks, work on a familiar task, such as summarizing a short article in your field. Make a first attempt without a dictionary. Note a few useful words and the main difficulty that isn’t about vocabulary. Work on these points, then try again with another article of similar difficulty.

Compare simple criteria: can you get the central idea across? Which words do you still need to look up? Is your summary understandable? Using a second text avoids confusing progress with having memorized the first one. Two weeks are mainly enough to adjust your method; they aren’t enough to say your level has changed.

If you have an official deadline, an initial assessment and a teacher’s advice can give your path a better structure. Also set aside time for the skills that will actually be assessed. Cards don’t replace interaction when your goal is to speak.

Frequently asked questions

Are the 500 to 600 hours for B2 added on top of B1?
No. The Cambridge table gives hours counted from the very beginning. It doesn’t provide a precise individual prediction between two levels.
