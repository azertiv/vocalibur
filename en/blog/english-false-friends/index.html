False friends in English: eight traps to fix in context

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • A false friend is checked in a sentence and a specific meaning.
  • A resemblance can be only partial: a false friend is not a forbidden word.
  • A useful correction explains what to use in the situation you have in mind.

Actually means “in fact” or “in reality”. The trap is to use it for the idea of “at present”, which French speakers express with actuellement, Spanish speakers with actualmente, Italian speakers with attualmente and German speakers with aktuell: in English, that idea calls for currently or at the moment. A false friend is a word whose resemblance to a word in your own language points you to the wrong meaning in a given situation; it isn’t an English word to ban.

Check the sentence before the label

English shares many borrowings and much inherited vocabulary with French, Spanish, Italian and German, but usage has developed differently in each language. So a similar form doesn’t guarantee the same meaning. Nor does it guarantee that the two words differ in absolutely every use.

Some pairs are almost always misleading in everyday use; others only partly so. For the French verb supporter, for example, support works when you back a team, but rarely when you mean putting up with a noise: English uses stand or put up with instead. The right question is still: what idea are you trying to express here?

Eight useful distinctions, with original examples

The links lead to the Cambridge Dictionary entries. The sentences below are examples written for this article, not quotations from the dictionary.

Resemblances to sort out in context
English wordMeaning illustratedExample
actuallyIn fact, in reality; not “at present” (French actuellement, Spanish actualmente, Italian attualmente, German aktuell).I thought it was closed, but it is actually open.
eventuallyIn the end, after some time; not “possibly” (French éventuellement, Spanish eventualmente, Italian eventualmente, German eventuell), which is possibly or if necessary.After several calls, we eventually found the address.
libraryA place where you borrow books; a shop that sells books (French librairie) is a bookshop or bookstore.I borrowed the novel from the library.
sensibleReasonable, showing good judgment; French sensible au bruit calls for sensitive to noise.Taking a map was a sensible decision.
attendTo be present at; French attendre is wait.Will you attend the meeting tomorrow?
assistTo help; French assister à a meeting is attend.A colleague assisted me with the report.
deceiveTo trick, to make someone believe something false; French décevoir calls for disappoint.The message was designed to deceive readers.
pass an examTo succeed in the exam; simply sitting it (French passer un examen) is take an exam.She took the exam in June and passed it.

Why one-to-one equivalents cause problems

For a French speaker, “délai = deadline” would be too simple, for example. A deadline, a waiting time and a period of time allowed are not the same thing.

The list is a starting point, not a complete map of meanings. Look at the pattern, the part of speech and the register. If a translation seems odd, check a full dictionary entry rather than concluding that the English word is never used that way.

An exercise that fixes the exact confusion

Try translating the French sentence Je travaille actuellement à Lyon. It describes a present situation: I currently work in Lyon or I’m working in Lyon at the moment can both work, depending on the context. Compare it with En fait, je travaille à Lyon, which can correct an assumption: Actually, I work in Lyon.

The difference isn’t down to a single word: the two sentences don’t play the same role in the conversation. Explaining that role can help you choose the right form next time.

Come back to the mistake without promising to erase it

A translation you understand today can still make you hesitate later. You can try to recall the answer before looking at it, check the context and plan to review the word later. Research on active recall supports this general principle; it doesn’t prove that any method “permanently rewrites” each wrong association.

When you keep mixing up two words, compare a few examples that show the difference. If both are entirely new, you might start by clarifying each use separately. The choice depends on the difficulty you notice; interleaving provides no universal rule for every pair.

Frequently asked questions

Can actually mean “in fact”?
Yes. The common mistake is to use it to mean “at present” (French actuellement, Spanish actualmente, Italian attualmente, German aktuell), which in English is currently or at the moment.
Does “pass an exam” mean sitting an exam?
This expression means succeeding in an exam. To say you sit it, without saying what the result was, use take an exam.
