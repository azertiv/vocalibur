Vocabulary in context or word lists: how to combine them

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Context and deliberate study are not mutually exclusive methods.
  • Percentages from two different meta-analyses don’t make up a comparative trial.
  • A correct answer on a word pair doesn’t measure everything you know about the word.

A bilingual list can be an effective way to link a form with a meaning. Reading can show a use that the list doesn’t cover. Setting them against each other, as if one made you learn and the other didn’t, hides the essential question: what are you doing with the words, and what do you want to know afterwards?

Context is not the opposite of deliberate study

You can learn words while focusing on the meaning of a text, without trying to memorize them: this is called incidental learning. You can also choose explicitly to work on them. A card with a sentence on it is still a deliberate activity if the aim is to learn its target word.

On the other hand, reading isn’t necessarily passive. Looking for a meaning, checking a hypothesis and connecting several sentences all take real work. So the format isn’t enough to classify an activity as “active” or “passive”.

What an experiment on repetition tells us

Webb (2007) studied 121 Japanese learners of English, with pseudowords met once, three times, seven times or ten times. Ten tests covered several dimensions of knowledge. More encounters improved at least some aspects, without showing that a fixed number of passes was enough to master everything.

This study shows why “knowing a word” is not a single result. On its own, it doesn’t settle the question between all forms of lists, cards and reading. Nor does it establish a universal rule of ten encounters.

Why comparing two meta-analyses can mislead

The reviews by Webb, Yanagisawa and Uchihara (2020) on word-focused activities and by Webb, Uchihara and Yanagisawa (2023) on incidental learning bring together different sets of studies.

Comparing their average gains directly doesn’t let you calculate the real advantage of cards over reading. Populations, tasks, exposures, delays and measures all differ. Without a suitable comparison of the time spent, you can’t work out a return “per minute” either. Finally, an average result after an activity doesn’t necessarily describe a single encounter with each word.

Their results can shed light on the conditions for learning. They don’t prove that a card in context is the best solution for everyone and every goal.

What a list brings

For a word such as narrow, linking it to its translation in your language can be a useful first step. A list lets you select what you want to work on and check an answer quickly. Hiding the English column and then trying to retrieve it turns the list into a recall tool.

But a short translation doesn’t cover everything. You may know narrow street without understanding a narrow victory. This second use calls for another shade of meaning. That is where an example or a dictionary adds information.

What context brings

The team won by a narrow margin. In this sentence, written for this example, the winning margin is small. The context shows a combination and a meaning that the image of a narrow street can’t explain on its own. It lets you connect the word to a use.

A context can also be too thin to allow an inference, or so difficult that it pulls your attention away from the target word. First understand the sentence, check the meaning, then ask yourself what it teaches you beyond the translation.

Combine activities without overloading yourself

Start from material you want to understand. Select a few useful words rather than every unknown one. For each, check the translation and look for a clear sentence. Then try to find the answer without having it in front of you, and come back to the material in another session.

Add some production when that is your goal: a written sentence, a spoken answer or a rephrasing. Have any uncertain wording checked. Success on a card isn’t enough to prove that a sentence you make up is idiomatic.

This routine is a teaching suggestion. Research on recall and spacing supports some of its principles, but the routine itself has not been evaluated, in Vocalibur or anywhere else.

Frequently asked questions

Should you give up vocabulary lists?
No. They can support a first link or targeted recall. Sentences and real uses add the dimensions that an isolated pair doesn’t show.
How many encounters are enough to know a word?
The studies give no universal number. The word, prior knowledge, the context and the type of test all change the results.
