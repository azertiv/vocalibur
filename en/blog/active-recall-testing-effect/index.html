Memory

Active recall: try before you look

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Good immediate performance doesn’t necessarily predict delayed retention.
  • In specific experiments, continuing to retrieve answers improves later recall.
  • Revealing a solution after trying can serve to check and correct.

Before you reveal a card, stop for a moment: can you find the answer? This attempt is what researchers call retrieval practice, or active recall. It is different from rereading information that is already in front of you. The testing effect is the benefit this retrieval can bring to a later test, beyond its role as an assessment.

The result depends on when you measure

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared studying and recalling short texts with students at Washington University. In their second experiment, 180 participants studied a text under three combinations of reading and tests without feedback. Repeated rereading gave the better result after five minutes; a week later, the group that had tested itself several times recalled more.

This reversal suggests you shouldn’t judge a method only by how easy it feels right after studying. It doesn’t mean rereading is useless: you first need to understand the material, and feedback is still needed when you get something wrong. The material, the students and the delays in this experiment don’t represent every way a language app is used.

The case of vocabulary: keep going after a first success

In Karpicke and Roediger (2008), 40 students learned 40 Swahili-English word pairs. After a first correct answer, some conditions kept testing the pair and others dropped it. On the test one week later, the conditions with repeated retrieval reached about 80% recall, against about a third in those that had stopped testing the words already answered correctly.

This result comes from a small sample and specific material; it is not a rate to expect in Vocalibur. Above all, it supports a concrete decision: a first success doesn’t prove that later returns have become useless. It doesn’t show that one recall is worth exactly ten rereadings, or that being able to translate these pairs guarantees a fluent conversation.

A gesture is not a mental operation

You can tap “Reveal” or swipe a card without having searched for the answer. The movement itself is therefore not active recall. The useful question is: what did you try to retrieve before seeing the solution?

In the same way, copying a word that is still visible is not the same task as writing it from memory. Both can be useful, but their results aren’t read in the same way. Reading a sentence aloud also trains something other than retrieving its missing word.

A short exercise with a real check

Read this sentence: She was reluctant to interrupt the meeting. Here, reluctant expresses reluctance, not being very willing to do something. Notice the pattern reluctant to + verb as well.

Then hide the word and its definition. Starting from the sentence’s translation in your own language (in French, “Elle était réticente à interrompre la réunion”), complete She was ___ to interrupt the meeting. Check the form and the meaning. Then change the situation slightly: how would you say that someone hesitates to ask for help? The aim is to produce reluctant to ask for help, rather than only reciting the first sentence.

This exercise is a teaching suggestion, not a calibrated test. Still, it separates three observable things: retrieving the form, understanding the nuance and using it in another pattern. Difficulty at the third step doesn’t cancel the success of the first two.

And when the answer doesn’t come?

A brief search can be useful; waiting indefinitely without any cue isn’t necessarily so. Look at the correction, clarify what was missing and come back later. If you had another plausible answer, check the difference in meaning rather than settling for a simple right or wrong verdict.

Research on recall and desirable difficulties doesn’t tell you to make every activity painful. The right level of difficulty depends on what you know and on the help available. An easy success is still a success, not proof of useless learning.

Frequently asked questions

Is getting it wrong enough to learn?
No. An unsuccessful attempt may need an explanation or a correction. Repeating a mistake without checking it is not the point of active recall.
Is one retrieval worth ten rereadings?
There is no universal ratio. The studies cover specific procedures, populations and delays; they establish no fixed equivalence between gestures.
