Memory

Spaced repetition: what the research shows

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Spacing describes the schedule; trying to retrieve describes the activity.
  • How useful an interval is depends in particular on when the test takes place.
  • A useful schedule has to stay compatible with your review workload.

Spaced repetition means coming back to the same material at several separate moments. In practice, it keeps time for words you have already met, instead of giving every session over to new ones. It doesn’t require every interval to get longer, and it doesn’t require an app to know the exact moment you will forget.

Three choices not to mix up

Choosing what to learn, deciding how to work on it and planning when to come back to it are three different problems. A list can select the words, a sentence can show how they are used, a card can ask you to recall and a schedule can space out the returns. None of these replaces the others.

You can space out simple rereading or, on the other hand, test yourself several times within a single session. Active recall and spacing are therefore not the same thing. They can be combined: try to find the word before looking at the answer, then do it again a few days later.

What the research lets us say

The 2006 meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues brings together 839 assessments from 317 experiments on verbal memory. It generally finds an advantage for distributed study on a delayed test. Above all, it shows an interaction: the best gap between sessions depends on the delay before the final test.

A meta-analysis combines different procedures, populations and measures. It gives neither a personal success rate nor a rhythm that suits every card. An average effect size can’t be turned into “this many more words learned in Vocalibur”.

In a 2008 experiment, the same team had people learn little-known facts in two sessions, then measured recall up to almost a year later. A gap that was too short or too long could be less helpful than an intermediate one. We explain this study, its figures and its limits in our article on the spacing effect.

A schedule is a proposal, not a law of memory

To see the logic, imagine you are learning reluctant. You come back to the word after a delay. If you retrieve it without help, the next return can be further away. If you mixed it up with another word, a closer return and a better example may help.

This example deliberately gives no number of days. The card’s history, your answer, the scheduler’s parameters and the limits of the session all play a part in the calculation. A sequence such as “1, 3, 7, 30 days” can work as a personal routine, but it shouldn’t be presented as the universal schedule established by research.

In the same way, constant spacing and expanding spacing are two possible arrangements. The finding that a distant horizon sometimes favors a longer gap between two sessions doesn’t show that every due date in a long series has to get longer.

Answers that really guide the schedule

In front of the card, look for what is being asked. Read the clue and the sentence, then try to find the missing English word before tapping “Reveal”. Then check. When several translations are possible, compare the meaning the sentence needs rather than a single isolated word in your own language.

A mistake sometimes needs a better explanation rather than one more identical repetition. For borrow and lend, ask yourself who receives the object and who gives it. A clear sentence can resolve a confusion that ten quick passes would leave untouched.

Make a mental note of what you had found before revealing the answer. An answer that becomes obvious once revealed wasn’t necessarily available before. On the other hand, a hesitation followed by correct recall is not the same as forgetting completely.

Keep the workload manageable

New words create future reviews. If the cards that are due already take up all your available time, cutting back on new words for a while can help more than always making sessions longer. Taking a break is not a teaching mistake, and there is no scientific daily quota. Our article on how many new words to learn suggests a way to adjust.

Vocalibur schedules reviews with FSRS-6, using the standard parameters, and keeps the history of each card. The schedule adapts to your answers, without measuring your whole level. A missed due date doesn’t cancel the history. Picking up again, answering honestly and finding a rhythm you can keep is more useful than trying to rebuild a perfect schedule.

Spaced repetition organizes the returns. Keep reading, listening, speaking and writing too: doing well on cards isn’t enough to prove you can use the language on your own.

Frequently asked questions

Should intervals always get longer?
No. The experiments don’t prescribe a single progression. Difficulty, your answers and how long you want to remember can justify different schedules.
Does spaced repetition replace practicing English?
No. It organizes how you come back to material. Understanding a conversation or writing a text also calls for activities suited to those tasks.
