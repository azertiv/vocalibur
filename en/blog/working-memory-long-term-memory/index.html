Memory

Working memory vs long-term memory

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Working memory helps hold and handle information during a task.
  • Its momentary capacity doesn’t give a daily maximum of new words.
  • An app statistic doesn’t directly measure biological consolidation.

You read an English sentence, keep the beginning in mind and work out the meaning of the end. A few minutes later, you have understood the idea but forgotten one particular word. To make sense of this gap, it helps to separate the mental work going on now from what stays available later.

Working memory: holding and handling information

The model by Baddeley and Hitch (1974) describes an active working memory, used to hold and process information during a task. Reading a complex sentence, comparing two translations or preparing an answer means coordinating several elements.

This model is not a photograph of the brain, and it doesn’t describe a small container in which each word has to wait to be transferred. Theories of memory have moved on since these founding models. They separate functions that help explain things, without imposing a single route (“sensory, short-term, working, long-term”) for everything you learn.

A sentence can be difficult because it contains several unknown words, a new pattern and an implicit reference. In that case the problem is not only the length of the sentence: it is the number of relationships you have to work out at the same time, with what you know now.

Momentary capacity is not a daily quota

The classic estimates of a few units held at once come from specific experimental tasks. A chunk is a grouped unit of information, and its size depends in particular on what you already know. For an experienced reader, an expression can be handled as a single unit; for a beginner, it can take several operations.

So it would be wrong to conclude that “you can only learn four words a day” or that “thirty words in a day necessarily overload memory”. A day contains many tasks one after another, and the ability to hold items at the same time is not the number of new things you can learn over time. Available time, tiredness, prior knowledge and future reviews also count.

Long-term memory: several kinds of knowledge

A word can become familiar without being available in every situation. You can understand reliable when reading, hesitate over its spelling, then not know whether it fits a device or a person. These are not three contradictory verdicts: you are testing different aspects of what you know.

To deepen your knowledge of a word, work on one specific aspect. In We need a reliable source of information, notice how it combines with source. In She is a reliable colleague, the word describes a person you can count on. Then compare with reliable equipment. These examples show usage; a single reading isn’t enough to make the word last.

Consolidation doesn’t show up in a counter

The review by Squire and colleagues (2015) distinguishes stabilization processes at different scales, cellular and systems-level. It also discusses several models of the roles of the hippocampus and the cortex. It is a synthesis of mechanisms, not a study showing how many days an English word takes to be “permanently acquired”.

Sleep and reactivation can contribute to how memories change. That doesn’t justify the literal image of a brain copying the day’s words into a permanent archive every night. An app measures neither this supposed transfer nor complete biological stabilization.

Reduce the difficulties that don’t help

If you get stuck on a card, start by identifying the cause. Is it the target word, the whole sentence, an unclear instruction or a confusion with another word? It can help to understand the sentence with its translation before attempting recall. Searching for a word in a sentence you can’t follow at all adds a difficulty you don’t necessarily need.

On the other hand, don’t remove every cue on principle. A short example can make the intended meaning clear; a cue that says too much can give the answer away. Adjusting this help is a teaching decision, not a contest to see who can make things hardest.

After checking, try to retrieve the word at another moment. Research on active recall and spacing supports the value of these returns, without imposing the same number of passes on every word.

Read the statistics with this distinction in mind

In Vocalibur, your reviews, the history of each card and your estimated vocabulary are different reference points. A card marked as known is not a word whose every use has been assessed. The memory bands reflect a stability estimated by the scheduler, not a neurological measurement.

Use these reference points to choose what you want to work on next. The most concrete check is still a real task: understanding a new sentence, retrieving the word without seeing it, or using it accurately. You will then know which aspect still needs attention.

Frequently asked questions

Can you only learn four words a day?
No. Measures of simultaneous capacity concern specific tasks. A day contains many successive chances to learn.
Is a word marked as known stored for good?
No. That status describes a decision or an estimate made by the tool. It doesn’t guarantee permanent recall in every situation.
