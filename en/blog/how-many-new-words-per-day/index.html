Method

How many new words should you learn per day?

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • New words create a future review workload.
  • Momentary memory capacity is not a daily learning limit.
  • The free plan’s limits are business rules, not an ideal teaching dose.

The right number of new words is the one that still leaves you time to review and use them. Research hasn’t established a universal daily quota. Ten, twenty or fifty new words can mean very different workloads depending on how familiar the words are, how precisely you need to know them and the reviews already planned.

Don’t turn working memory into a daily ceiling

The work of Cowan (2001) estimates that we can only keep about four items in mind at once, in specific experimental tasks. This momentary capacity doesn’t tell you how many words someone can learn over a day made up of many tasks one after another.

You don’t have to keep thirty words in your head at the same moment to study them one after the other. The real practical problem may be the time needed, attention, the difficulty of the examples or the volume of reviews those new words will create. A limit of “four units” can’t be used to calculate a quota of cards.

Count the returns too

Introducing a word generally creates several future occasions to work on it. The Anki manual warns about this build-up: increasing new cards quickly can make reviews hard to keep up with. This is practical advice, not a scientific rule.

To make this cost concrete, here is an example calculation that is entirely made up. If your session contains ten new words that take thirty seconds each, discovering them takes five minutes. After a few months at this pace, the reviews due can reach about a hundred a day: at ten seconds each, they add almost seventeen minutes. The total then goes beyond twenty minutes, before breaks or explanations. Your real times may be very different; the point of the calculation is to include both categories.

So a number of cards is not a length of time. A card you recognize immediately and a card where you examine three meanings don’t take the same work.

A way to adjust based on your own practice

Start with a realistic time budget, for example about ten minutes if that slot suits you. In Vocalibur, the daily goal can be counted in minutes rather than cards for exactly this reason. Keep the same small number of new words for several sessions so that you can see their reviews appear.

Simply note whether you can finish without rushing, whether you understand the corrections and whether the cards due stay manageable. If you regularly run out of time, reduce the new words or pause them for a few days. If the session stays comfortable and you want to do more, increase gradually.

Don’t change everything at once: quota, length, difficulty of the words and the way you answer. Otherwise you won’t know what changed the workload. It isn’t a scientific experiment, but it is enough to find a pace you can keep.

A new word isn’t always entirely new

You may know develop and discover development, recognize charge but learn a new meaning, or meet an expression in which every single word is familiar. These situations don’t take the same effort.

Also decide what you want to achieve: understanding the meaning when reading, writing the word without help, or using it in a sentence. Research on receptive and productive knowledge shows why a single counter can’t sum up all these skills.

Where spacing fits in

The experiments reviewed by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) support spreading encounters out over time, with effects that depend on the final delay and the task. They don’t show that twenty new words would be better than ten, or that you must review every day without exception.

Their implication for your quota is indirect: keep room for returns instead of spending all your time on discovery. A missed recall calls for a correction and another chance; it is not a reason to add words to push a counter up.

Vocalibur’s limits are not a prescription

On the free plan, Vocalibur lets you work on up to 50 different cards a day, new words included, of which 15 new words at most (up to 50 on the first day). These are plan limits, not targets to reach. Premium removes these daily limits; that doesn’t mean that learning without limits is a good idea for everyone.

You can do less, take a break and pick up again. If your reviews already fill your session, spending your time on words you have already started is learning in its own right. The useful setting is the one you can sustain while understanding your answers, not the maximum the interface allows.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a number of new words per day that works for everyone?
No. What you already know, the difficulty of the words and your review workload change the work needed. The limit on Vocalibur’s free plan, 15 new words a day (up to 50 on the first day), is a plan rule, not a prescription.
What should you do if reviews pile up?
Reduce new words for a while and watch the real workload. If a card stays difficult, clarify the meaning or the confusion instead of just adding more repetitions.
