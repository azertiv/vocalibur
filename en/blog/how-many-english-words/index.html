How many words do you need to know in English?

4 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • A word form, a lemma, a word family and a meaning are not the same unit.
  • Text coverage counts occurrences, not a reader’s full abilities.
  • Nation’s estimates depend on a coverage target and on specific corpora.

There is no number of words that would be enough to “speak English”. Reading a technical article, following a conversation and telling someone about an event call for different knowledge. A figure only becomes useful once you say which unit is being counted, what the task is and which texts are involved.

Three units behind the word “word”

Counting word forms means counting each spelling: help, helps and helped are three forms. A lemma groups together the inflected forms of a single entry. A word family can also include derived words considered transparent enough, such as helpful and unhelpful, depending on the rules of the classification.

Knowing the base word doesn’t guarantee that all its derived forms are understood. Choosing the family as the unit rests on an assumption about knowledge of word formation. You can’t directly compare a card counter, an estimate in lemmas and a result in families.

Meanings add another dimension: knowing bank in its financial sense doesn’t tell you whether you understand it as the side of a river (in Vocalibur, these two homonyms each have their own card). So the number of entries doesn’t capture the full depth of a vocabulary.

Lexical coverage counts occurrences

Coverage is the share of the running words in a text that the reader knows, or that the reference list contains, depending on the method used. If the words covered account for 98 occurrences out of 100, coverage is 98%. A word repeated ten times counts ten times in this calculation; it doesn’t become ten different entries.

In a passage of 500 running words, 95% coverage leaves 25 occurrences uncovered; 98% leaves 10. This calculation illustrates the proportions without predicting comprehension. Unknown words concentrated in one key sentence can get in the way more than the same number spread elsewhere.

Nation’s benchmarks, read correctly

Nation (2006) analyzes how well frequency lists cover several corpora. Taking 98% as the target for comprehension without help, he estimates that about 8,000 to 9,000 word families are needed for the written texts studied and 6,000 to 7,000 for speech. Proper nouns and the type of text play a part in the calculations.

The wording is conditional. It is not a trial showing that everyone automatically knows these families at a certain level, or that reaching the total guarantees understanding any novel or conversation. The lists, the corpora and command of derived forms all affect the estimate.

98% is not a comprehension switch

Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe (2011) studied the link between vocabulary knowledge and comprehension with 661 participants from eight countries, using two texts. They observe a broadly continuous relationship, with no sharp threshold at which comprehension suddenly takes off.

This is a measured relationship between variables, not an intervention guaranteeing that adding one word produces a set gain in comprehension. Syntax, knowledge of the topic, reasoning, speed of speech and pronunciation also play a part. You can start reading with some help before you reach high coverage.

What about levels A1 to C2?

The CEFR describes communication abilities. It provides no official conversion between a number of words and an overall level. The distributions observed with a vocabulary test depend on its population, its ceiling and its unit of counting.

A score capped at the most frequent words doesn’t necessarily measure all the vocabulary of an advanced learner. A correlation with levels doesn’t let anyone calculate how many cards are missing to “get B2”. That is why the levels Vocalibur shows from your estimated vocabulary are approximate reference points, not official CEFR thresholds. Our article on CEFR levels explains this distinction.

Choose a target that is useful to you

Start from a use: reading articles in your field, following a particular series or explaining your work. Then look at what is missing. A word that is rare in the language as a whole can be very frequent in your work. On the other hand, a word ranked high in a list may already be familiar to you.

Choose a few useful entries, work on them in sentences and go back to the original material. Can you now understand the passage? Then check with another text. This approach doesn’t replace a standardized test, but it links learning to a concrete decision.

Reading Vocalibur’s figures

Vocalibur separates the words you have acquired, shown in the middle of the badge on Today, from your estimated vocabulary, which starts from the words you said you knew when you set up the app. The estimate comes with some uncertainty; it is neither an exact count of all your words nor a certificate. In the same way, when Vocalibur picks out the words from its catalog in a text to create a personal deck, that number doesn’t tell you how much of the text you understand.

Use the counter to follow one aspect of your work, then confirm your progress in reading, listening and producing language. The aim is to widen what you can understand and express, rather than to own an abstract number of words.

Frequently asked questions

Does knowing 98% of the words guarantee you understand a text?
No. Grammar, knowledge of the topic, the missing words and the reading task also count. Coverage is not a comprehension switch.
How many words do you need for B2?
The CEFR sets no official word threshold. Empirical correspondences depend on the tests, the populations and the unit counted; the levels Vocalibur shows are approximate reference points too.
