Memory

Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: what it really shows

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Ebbinghaus measured the work saved when relearning syllables, not the percentage of English words forgotten.
  • The 2015 replication also involves a single person.
  • Reviews set up chances to recall without predicting the exact moment something is forgotten.

You understood an English word yesterday, but today you can’t bring it back. That doesn’t mean you have “a bad memory”, or that learning it was a waste of time. Understanding a word, recognizing it and retrieving it without help are different tasks. The forgetting curve helps you think about what happens between learning and recall, as long as you don’t turn it into a personal countdown.

What Ebbinghaus actually measured

At the end of the 19th century, Hermann Ebbinghaus studied lists of meaningless syllables, using himself as the subject. After a delay, he learned them again. His central measure was savings in relearning: how much work could he save compared with the first round of learning? His 1885 book, translated into English in 1913 as Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology, describes this approach.

This measure is not the percentage of words you could recite. If a list first took ten units of work, then six to relearn it, the savings would be 40%. This arithmetic example is made up. It shows why writing “you have forgotten 60% of your vocabulary” on the basis of 40% savings would be a misreading.

The historical experiments made one important idea visible: memory performance can drop sharply at first, then more slowly. They don’t give a universal schedule for your English words. Isolated syllables, a sentence you understood and a word you have already met at work don’t have the same familiarity or the same retrieval cues.

A useful replication, with a major limit

In 2015, Murre and Dros repeated the method. One participant, Dros, spent about 70 hours on 69 lists, relearned after delays ranging from 20 minutes to 31 days. The general shape of the decline appeared again, with some irregularities.

This is a detailed replication of a procedure, not a representative estimate for the whole population. The authors discuss in particular a rise around 24 hours, which fits several explanations, including consolidation. The experiment doesn’t manipulate sleep, so it doesn’t show that sleeping causes this rise. Differences in procedure from Ebbinghaus and the way the lists were organized also limit exact comparisons.

Why a word can come back with a cue

Take bridge. You may no longer find its translation when the word stands alone, then understand We crossed the bridge before sunset. straight away. The sentence gives you cues. You may also retrieve its translation in your own language without knowing how to use bridge the gap, which calls for a different kind of knowledge about usage.

To follow your learning, make the question precise: finding the meaning of the written word, finding its English form from a cue, or using it in a new sentence? Success in one direction doesn’t automatically certify the others. We look at this distinction in more detail in Recognizing a word and producing it.

The practical consequence: plan chances to come back

The historical curve doesn’t say “review after exactly twenty minutes”. Experiments on spacing study the distribution of encounters directly. They show that the useful gap depends, among other things, on when you still want to remember. Spacing means neither reviewing every word every day nor waiting for failure at all costs.

A simple practice is to search briefly for the answer, check it, then come back later. If the word doesn’t come, reread its meaning and its sentence instead of searching endlessly. If you find it easily, good: that is also useful information for the next review.

The point to remember: forgetting an answer doesn’t necessarily erase everything you have learned. The aim is to organize useful returns without turning learning into constant monitoring, rather than to prevent all forgetting.

Frequently asked questions

Do we forget a fixed percentage after one day?
No. The material, prior knowledge, the delay and the test all change the result. The historical values are not a personal curve.
Was a forgotten card useless?
No. Difficulty recalling something doesn’t let you conclude that every trace has gone. A cue or relearning can reveal partial knowledge.
