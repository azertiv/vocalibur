Spacing effect: how long to wait between reviews?

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • The gap between sessions and the delay before the test are two different variables.
  • The best gap tested in one experiment is not a universal personal optimum.
  • A relative gain in recall should not be read as percentage points.

Two separate sessions and two almost back-to-back sessions can produce different results, even when they cover the same material. This is the spacing effect. The interesting question goes beyond “should I space my reviews?” to “what gap was tested, for what final delay, with what measure?”.

Two different lengths of time

The gap between sessions separates the first study session from the review. The retention interval separates that review from the final test. Mixing them up leads to bad recipes: an experiment with two sessions followed by a test is not a complete review program for a whole year.

The review by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) shows that these two lengths of time act together. The advantage of a given gap depends in particular on when memory is assessed. So there is no reason to treat the longest possible interval as always better.

A large-scale experiment

In Cepeda et al. (2008), 1,354 participants from an online panel learned 32 little-known facts. Their average age was 34 and 72% were women. The researchers assigned participants to different combinations of gap and final delay, with recall tests followed by recognition tests.

In the first learning session, each answer had to be produced correctly once. The second session included two tests with feedback. For final recall, the best gaps among those tested were the following:

Gaps observed in the two-session procedure of Cepeda et al. (2008)
Delay after the second sessionBest gap tested before that session
7 days1 day
35 days11 days
70 days21 days
350 days21 days

This table is not a prescription for your cards. The recognition results didn’t have exactly the same best gaps. Estimates drawn from a fitted curve can also differ from the best point actually tested.

What does the 64% figure mean?

Pooling their comparisons, the authors report a relative advantage of 64% in recall for the best gap compared with a gap of almost zero. That means neither 64 percentage points nor 64% more words learned by anyone who uses spaced repetition.

To tell the units apart, take a made-up example: going from 25 to 40 correct answers out of 100 is 15 percentage points, but a relative increase of 60%. These two numbers describe the same difference, with different denominators. None of these made-up scores comes from Cepeda’s experiment.

The number of repetitions was fixed, but not the exact length of each session: the first one continued until each fact had been answered correctly once. The long delays also led to more dropouts. Finally, general-knowledge facts studied twice are not thousands of words met while reading and reviewed over several months.

Why this result is still useful

It shows that, with the same number of passes, the timing of the return can change the result a great deal. It also gives a reason to be wary of fixed schedules sold as “scientifically optimal”. The right moment depends on the task, on what you already know and on what you still want to know later.

Theoretical explanations involve several mechanisms, including retrieval from memory and variation in the conditions of encoding. Saying “effort always consolidates” would be too simple. A word that has become completely incomprehensible first needs a new explanation; growing difficulty doesn’t guarantee growing benefit.

A reasonable everyday application

Set aside moments to come back to words you have already met. Before looking at the solution, try to answer. Check the meaning and the form, then let an interval pass that suits your routine or your scheduler. The schedule can get shorter after a difficulty or longer after a success; it doesn’t have to trace a perfect progression.

A short session is a practical way to fit these returns in; no study shows it is better in every situation. If you learn better at another time of day or in longer sessions, what matters is still not to confuse focused work with long-term retention.

Frequently asked questions

What does the relative gain of 64% mean?
It compares recall results in certain conditions of Cepeda’s 2008 experiment. It is neither 64 percentage points nor a gain demonstrated in Vocalibur.
Does the table give me my next schedule?
No. It sums up the best gaps tested in one procedure using little-known facts. It doesn’t prescribe the due dates of each of your words.
