How to learn English vocabulary: a method in 5 steps

5 min readReviewed November 28, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Start with the most frequent words: 1,000 words make up 82% of an ordinary text.
  • Learn each word in a sentence, and look for the answer before reading it.
  • Space your reviews and keep a modest pace, every day.

Learning English vocabulary takes less willpower than people think, and more method. Research on memory and on language learning points to five habits: pick the most useful words, meet them in sentences, look for the answer before reading it, space your reviews, and keep a steady pace. Here is each one, what it brings, and where it stops.

1. Start with the most frequent words

Words are not equal. A few hundred keep coming back (the, have, because, enough), while thousands of others appear once in a whole book. According to Vocalibur’s calculation on books and film dialogue, the 1,000 most frequent words make up 82% of the words in an ordinary text, and the 3,000 most frequent 92%. Learning in that order means recognizing as many words as possible for every hour you put in.

These orders of magnitude match Nation (2006): reading without help takes several thousand word families, and each extra thousand brings less than the one before. Recognizing 8 words out of 10 is not enough to understand a text: it is a starting point, not a finish line. The list of the 1,000 most common English words shows where that start begins, and the article how many words you need to know in English details the figures.

2. Learn each word in a sentence

A word on its own is hard to remember and even harder to use. In a sentence, it comes with its precise meaning, its usual neighbours and its pattern: “depend on”, “make a decision”. Lists and cards help fix the form; context fixes the use. The two work together, as the article vocabulary in context or lists explains.

The meta-analysis by Webb, Yanagisawa and Uchihara (2020) shows that deliberate learning activities, flashcards included, bring clear gains, faster than reading alone. Reading a lot is still essential: it is where a learned word settles.

3. Look for the word before you check the answer

Rereading a list feels like mastery; forcing yourself to retrieve the word, even when you fail, makes it stick better. In the experiment by Karpicke and Roediger (2008), students learned Swahili-English word pairs: those who kept testing themselves recalled about 80% a week later, against a little over a third for those who only studied them again.

In practice: look at the clue, search for the word, and only then reveal the answer. Hesitating is not failing: the effort is what consolidates. The article active recall: try before you look gives the conditions.

4. Space your reviews

Going over a word ten times in one evening keeps it until the next day; going over it four times across a month keeps it far longer. The review by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) confirms that spaced reviews beat massed ones, and shows that the right gap depends on when you need to remember.

Working out those gaps by hand is tedious. Spaced repetition algorithms do it for every word, from your answers: a word that slips away comes back sooner, a word you know moves further out. Vocalibur uses FSRS-6, described in the article FSRS: how the next review is calculated.

5. Keep a modest pace, every day

Each new word creates reviews for the weeks that follow. Adding fifty words one day and nothing for a week produces a mountain of reviews and a lot of forgetting. About ten new words a day, with that day’s reviews, fits in a short daily session and adds up fast: more than 3,000 words in a year. The article how many new words to learn a day helps you choose your pace.

Frequently asked questions

How many English words should I learn a day?
About ten new words a day, with that day’s reviews, fits in a short daily session and passes 3,000 words in a year. More is possible, but reviews pile up quickly.
Should I learn vocabulary by theme or by frequency?
By frequency for the basics, because the most common words are useful everywhere. Themes help afterwards for a specific need: a trip, a job, an exam.
