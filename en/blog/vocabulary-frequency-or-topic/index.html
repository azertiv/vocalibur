Learn vocabulary by frequency or by topic?

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • Frequency depends on the corpus used to measure it.
  • A topic of use and a category of similar words are not the same.
  • Your choice should respond to your own material and to the confusions you notice.

Frequency helps you pick the words likely to come up often. A topic helps you pick the ones that matter in a given situation. The two criteria can work together. The risk appears when one of them becomes an absolute rule: following any list in order, or learning all similar words together.

Frequency describes a corpus, not your life

The analyses by Nation (2006) show that very frequent words cover a large share of the running words in the texts studied. But the order depends on the corpus: conversation, news, fiction and specialized documents don’t distribute words in exactly the same way.

For a beginner, common words can be useful in many situations. For someone who already knows them, reviewing them systematically isn’t necessarily a priority. And a professional term that is rare in a general corpus can come up every day in that person’s exchanges.

Frequency is therefore a good starting point, to be adjusted to what you really know and how you want to use the language. It measures neither the CEFR level of a word in each of its meanings nor its value to you personally.

A topic and a category are not the same thing

A list of fruit brings together words from the same category: pear, peach, plum. A travel scene can bring together different kinds of words: ticket, depart, delayed, platform. Both sets could be labeled a “topic”, but they don’t create the same relationships.

The work of Tinkham (1997) separates these semantic and thematic groupings in vocabulary learning. It suggests not assuming that a list ordered by category will always be easier to learn. The context and the procedure, however, limit how far this can be generalized.

A recent experiment shows why the nuance matters

Pérez-Serrano and colleagues (2022) had 102 Spanish-speaking students watch a video pairing twelve Spanish words with pseudowords, seen once, four times or eight times. Half of the targets were grouped in a category of fruit.

For exact recall, the overall effect of semantic similarity was not significant; its interaction with the number of encounters was. Unrelated words improved at each step, while the fruit words improved between one and four encounters, then hardly at all after that: at eight exposures, the unrelated words were clearly better recalled (56% against 43%). The authors point out two limits themselves: no delayed test, and targets that were not rotated across conditions.

The result points to a risk of confusion in this setup. It doesn’t show that learning two fruits on the same day is always bad, or that an app can remove interference with a simple sort.

Choose according to the problem you want to solve

If you are meeting a lot of new words, starting with the most frequent ones gives you a broad base. If you are preparing for a specific situation, choose the words you will need instead. If your difficulty is a confusion between two terms, take the time to compare how they are used.

For example, to take a train, platform and departure meet an immediate need. To tell trip and journey apart, a simple “travel” label isn’t enough: you need to look at what each word refers to in the sentence. The comparison can come after you have first understood each one separately.

These are practical guidelines, not a proven optimal order. You can adjust them by watching whether the words come back in what you read and listen to, and whether you tell them apart better.

Putting your reading to concrete use

Choose an English article you really want to read. Note the words that block an important idea or that come up several times. Check their meaning, then select a small set to work on. You don’t need to turn every unknown term into a card.

When a word is rare but central to your field, keep it. When it is secondary and unlikely to come back, you can leave it aside. Sorting is part of the work: writing everything down is not the same as making progress.

Frequently asked questions

Is a list of similar words always easier?
No. Words from the same category can compete with each other in some tasks. Results depend on the procedure and the number of encounters.
When should you favor a personal topic?
When it responds to real reading, a real conversation or real work. General frequency is still a useful guide, but it doesn’t know all your needs.
