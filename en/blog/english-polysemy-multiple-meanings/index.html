English words with multiple meanings: polysemy

3 min readReviewed October 26, 2026Method and corrections

Translated and adapted from French by the Vocalibur team. Sources are cited in their original language.

Key points

  • The written form of a word isn’t enough to identify its meaning.
  • A measure of fast recognition is not a teaching recipe.
  • Knowing an entry doesn’t mean mastering each of its senses.

You know bank as a place that keeps money, then you read They sat on the river bank. The sentence isn’t about a financial institution but about the side of a river. A familiar form can carry a meaning you don’t know yet. That is one reason not to reduce vocabulary to a count of entries.

One form, several relationships between meanings

Polysemy refers to related meanings; homonymy, to meanings generally analyzed as separate under the same form. Where the line falls can depend on the analysis and the dictionary. It isn’t always obvious to learners, and you don’t need to settle it to understand a sentence.

With bark, the outer layer of a tree and the sound a dog makes don’t call for the same mental picture. With head, the body part and some figurative uses can be related. A resemblance between meanings can help you, but looking for a shared story at all costs can also produce a false explanation.

Work on a distinction you can observe

Compare these original sentences, using the Cambridge Dictionary entry for bank if needed:

The sentence shows which meaning is intended
English exampleMeaning in this example
She deposited the money at the bank.The financial institution.
The path follows the river bank.The edge of the river.
We cannot bank on good weather.To count on something.

The last example contains the pattern bank on. Knowing bank and on separately isn’t enough to work out its meaning. The grammatical form and the neighboring words are part of what you learn.

What the research measures

Psycholinguistic experiments, such as those by Rodd, Gaskell and Marslen-Wilson (2002), look at how people recognize words that have several related senses or several separate meanings. A difference in speed on a lexical decision task doesn’t directly measure how well a card method works.

So you can’t conclude that related meanings “always reinforce each other”, or that you should wait a set number of days before tackling a second meaning. Going from a recognition task to a teaching recommendation requires an extra step of verification.

González-Fernández and Webb (2024) study learning with cards more directly: 36 learners, compared with a control group of 30, worked on additional meanings of familiar words and on first meanings of unknown words, with recall and recognition measured immediately and then a week later. The result: learning a new meaning of a familiar word turned out to be as difficult as learning the first meaning of an unknown word. So knowing the form doesn’t guarantee that a secondary meaning will be easier to learn.

This lack of difference doesn’t prove, however, that every new meaning takes exactly the same effort as a brand-new word.

A method for not learning everything at once

When a new meaning appears in something you are reading, start by checking in a dictionary which meaning it is. Make a mental note of what sets it apart from the meaning you already know. A translation and a short sentence can be enough for this first clarification.

Then try another example of the same use. Can you explain why the usual translation doesn’t fit? Then switch to an example of the familiar meaning to check the distinction. This is a practical suggestion, not a rule taken from the studies.

You don’t need to study every use listed in a dictionary before using the word. Frequency and your own material can guide what you pick. A meaning that is rare but important for your work may deserve more attention than a frequent one in a field you never come across.

Reading Vocalibur’s counters correctly

In Vocalibur, two homonyms such as bank (“financial institution”) and bank (“riverside”) are two separate cards, each with its own reviews. Related meanings of the same word, on the other hand, share a card and a due date; when you reveal the card, you also see its other meanings. So a number of acquired words doesn’t mean that all their uses are known.

If a word surprises you in a new sentence, that isn’t necessarily a step backwards. You may have discovered a side of it that your first encounter didn’t cover. Go back to the context and check the usage: deepening a familiar word counts just as much in your practice as discovering a new form.

Frequently asked questions

Should you learn all the meanings of a word at once?
Not necessarily. Start with the meaning that matters in your material, then separate out a new sense when you meet it. The studies cited impose no fixed delay.
Does Vocalibur track the memory of each meaning separately?
Partly. Two homonyms, such as bank (“financial institution”) and bank (“riverside”), are two separate cards, each with its own reviews. Related meanings of the same word share a card and a schedule; when you reveal the card, you also see its other meanings.
